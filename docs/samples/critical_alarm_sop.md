# Critical Alarm Management and Escalation SOP

## Purpose and scope
This standard operating procedure defines how bedside monitoring alarms are triaged, responded to,
escalated, and documented on a monitored ward. It applies to every alarm raised by a bedside
monitor or by the automated arrhythmia relay, for every patient under continuous monitoring. This
is a **synthetic sample document** used to exercise the knowledge-base upload path; it is not
clinical guidance and must not be used for patient care.

## Alarm categories
Alarms are classified into three categories that determine the response time. A **critical** alarm
indicates an immediately life-threatening rhythm or a physiological state incompatible with
survival, such as ventricular fibrillation, pulseless ventricular tachycardia, or asystole. A
**high-priority** alarm indicates a clinically significant arrhythmia or deteriorating vital signs
that require prompt review, such as atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, sustained
tachycardia, or a Modified Early Warning Score of 4 or more. An **advisory** alarm indicates a
technical or low-acuity condition, such as a displaced lead, low battery, or a signal-quality
warning, and does not by itself indicate patient deterioration.

## Immediate response in the first sixty seconds
Attend the bedside within 30 seconds of a critical alarm and within 2 minutes of a high-priority
alarm. Confirm the patient is responsive and check for a pulse before trusting the monitor. Verify
that the rhythm on the monitor matches the patient's clinical state, because lead displacement and
motion artefact are the two most common causes of a spurious critical alarm. Do not silence an
alarm before the patient has been assessed. If the patient is unresponsive and pulseless, call the
arrest team immediately and begin the resuscitation protocol without waiting for confirmation of
the rhythm.

## Escalation checklist
Work through these steps in order for every alarm that survives bedside confirmation.
1. Confirm the patient and bed identity against the alarm record, with the reported event type and
   the time it was raised.
2. Record the vital signs at the time of the alarm: heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate,
   oxygen saturation, and temperature. Calculate the Modified Early Warning Score.
3. Classify the alarm as confirmed, a technical artefact, or uncertain, and record the reason.
4. Escalate using the ladder below if the alarm is confirmed or uncertain. An alarm you cannot
   positively classify as an artefact is escalated, not dismissed.
5. Acknowledge the alarm in the monitoring system, naming the responder and the action taken.
6. Document the assessment, the escalation, and the response received within 15 minutes.

## Escalation ladder and response times
Escalate to the responsible nurse in charge for any confirmed high-priority alarm, expecting a
bedside response within 10 minutes. Escalate to the on-call clinician for any confirmed critical
alarm, for a Modified Early Warning Score of 6 or more, or for any single vital sign scoring 3,
expecting a response within 5 minutes. Escalate directly to the rapid response or critical care
outreach team for a cardiac arrest rhythm, an unprotected airway, or a patient who remains unstable
after the first intervention, expecting an immediate response. A rising trend escalates one level:
if the Modified Early Warning Score increases by 2 or more points between consecutive observations,
escalate to the on-call clinician even when the absolute score is not yet critical.

## Failed escalation
If the contacted responder does not arrive within the expected response time, escalate to the next
level of the ladder immediately rather than repeating the same call. Two unanswered attempts at any
level constitute a failed escalation. Record the failure, the times of both attempts, and the level
escalated to, and notify the nurse in charge on the same shift. A failed escalation is a reportable
patient-safety incident and is reviewed at the weekly governance meeting.

## Acknowledgement and documentation
Every alarm is acknowledged by a named responder, and the acknowledgement is recorded with a
timestamp. An acknowledgement asserts that a clinician has assessed the patient, not merely that
the sound was silenced. The record must state the reported event type, whether it was confirmed or
classified as a false positive, the vital signs at the time, the action taken, and the escalation
level reached. Unacknowledged critical alarms older than 15 minutes are listed on the shift handover
and reviewed by the nurse in charge.

## False positives and alarm fatigue
A confirmed technical artefact is documented as a false positive with the cause where it is known,
most often lead displacement, patient movement, or electrical interference. Persistent false alarms
on the same bed are addressed the same shift by re-siting the electrodes and reviewing the alarm
limits, because alarm fatigue is a direct patient-safety risk. Alarm limits are reviewed for every
patient at the start of each shift and after any change in clinical condition. Alarm limits are
never widened solely to reduce alarm volume; any change to limits is recorded with the clinical
reason and the name of the clinician who authorised it.

## Handover
At every shift handover, review all unacknowledged alarms, every critical alarm raised in the
preceding 12 hours, all outstanding action items arising from those alarms, and any bed with
repeated false alarms. The outgoing nurse in charge confirms that each critical alarm has a
documented outcome before the handover is complete.
